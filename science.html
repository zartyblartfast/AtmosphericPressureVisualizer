Why Does Atmospheric Pressure Decrease with Altitude?

Atmospheric pressure is the force produced by the weight of the air above a given point. At sea level, there is a tall column of atmosphere above you. As you climb, part of that column is below you, so the air above weighs less and the pressure falls.

There is no sharp vacuum boundary. Gas becomes progressively more rarefied as altitude increases. The Kármán line at 100 km is a widely used conventional boundary of space; molecules still exist above it, but collisions become much less frequent and the atmosphere gradually approaches near-vacuum conditions.

Why is the curve not a straight line?

Air is densest near Earth's surface because gravity concentrates more molecules there. A modest increase in altitude therefore removes a relatively large amount of air from the column above. Higher up, the air is already very thin, so the absolute pressure change becomes smaller.

Temperature also matters

Temperature affects how spread out the air is. The atmosphere is divided into layers with different temperature trends, so the visualizer uses a simplified atmospheric model rather than one constant rate of decrease. The pressure curve is therefore shaped by both gravity and the temperature profile.

What the visualizer calculates

Entered altitude is geometric height above sea level, from 0 to 100 km. Up to 86 km, the calculator converts it to geopotential height and integrates pressure continuously through seven temperature-gradient layers. From 86 to 100 km, a reference pressure-ratio fit is scaled to the calculated 86 km pressure. The result is calculated internally in hPa and can then be displayed in the pressure unit you choose. The chart's broad coloured atmosphere bands are schematic, not exact model-layer boundaries.

Changing sea-level temperature offsets the whole lower temperature profile and reintegrates its pressure: an educational pressure-response assumption, not a standard aviation ISA delta-T implementation or a measured weather forecast. Above 86 km, this changes only the boundary-pressure scaling; the reference upper pressure ratio remains unchanged, with no custom upper-temperature model. Humidity and actual weather are not modelled. See the detailed calculation for equations, reference checks, and limitations.
